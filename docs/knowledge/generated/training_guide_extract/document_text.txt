PRODUCTION DIRECTOR TRAINING PROGRAM
NAME:                                                          START DATE:
The purpose of this program is to teach you the tasks that are required of each department under Division 4 here at the Erin Hanson Gallery.
It is very important that you follow the instructions of the training program exactly, and that you ask the Production Manager when you have any problems trying to get it done. If you encounter something that isn’t covered in these instructions, don’t try to figure it out on your own. The Production Manager can refer you to the correct section of these instructions if you’ve missed something, or clarify any questions that aren’t covered here.
IMPORTANT:
Speed is an important part of workshop productivity. However, speed comes with practice. For this training program, it is more important that you do each task CORRECTLY, than do them QUICKLY. After you learn to do the tasks well, you’ll get faster and faster the more that you do them. But for now, focus on quality rather than quantity.
HOW TO DO THIS PROGRAM
This program is organized in sections. Each section covers one part of the workshop’s production.Within each section, you will:
Read the specific instructions for a task.
Watch the Production Manager (or another experienced employee) perform that task.
Perform that task yourself while being supervised, and do this until you can perform that task adequately.
Perform that task a few more times without direct supervision. Afterwards, you’ll get the approval of the Production Manager (or supervising employee) on what you did, or issue corrections if you made any mistakes.
Each step is initialed when complete. You initial the steps you do, and the Production Manager (abbreviated as Prod Mgr) will initial on steps where she has checked your work.
SEQUENCE
The sections of this checklist don’t have to be done in sequence. You should go to the Production Manager and find out from her what section to work on next. She will direct you based on what is needed in the workshop at the time. For example, if there are several prints that need frames made for them, she will have you work on the framing section. If a print run needs to happen, she’ll have you work on the printer section. She’ll keep assigning you sections until the whole checklist is completed.
However, within each section, it’s extremely important that you follow the steps of that section in sequence. Don’t go skipping around the steps.
Stats and Schedule
Stats
We have specific metrics that we track in the warehouse that do aid us in managing our production line. We will go over each of these metrics and how they can help us run the warehouse at top efficiency.
Metrics:
$ Value of Services Delivered (VSD)
% Orders Shipped on Deadline
# Particles Handled
# Warehouse Operator Steps
Definitions:
Value of Services Delivered is the dollar amount of artwork (originals, 3D
prints, canvas prints) delivered to customers that week. To count as Services
Delivered, the artwork must be confirmed to have reached the customer. For
prints, FedEx confirmation is acceptable. For originals, we require the
customer’s signature on the order.
This metric gives us information regarding the value of the services that are being shipped directly to customers, not items that are needed for gallery use. This metric is partly controlled by us and partly controlled by sales. Without the sales associates making the sales, we do not have products with a paid value being shipped out. We do have control over if these products are being shipped on time, and the original line. On weeks we may have lower production, we can see if there are originals that have been sold that could be shipped out to raise our VSD.
Orders Shipped on Deadline is a percentage stat. There are various
deadlines to get an order shipped (3 days for originals, 14 days for prints,
etc., and all modified if the customer wishes to put a delay on the order). The percentage of orders shipped on deadline in a week gives you this stat. If an
item is over deadline, it is counted as a negative on the weekly stat every
week it is in the shop, not just the week it finally gets shipped out.
This metric we have complete control over in the warehouse. The Erin Hanson Gallery makes a guarantee to our customers that artwork will be sent out on deadline, meaning that depending on the product we have to adhere to the deadline that is in place for it. The only time this rule can be adjusted for customers, is if they give us a specific date that they want to have the artwork shipped by or picked up. In this case, we can adjust the due date to what will work best for the customer's request. Customers are relying on us to receive their artwork by a specific deadline and we want to uphold this standard.
Particles Handled is the number of finished products that leave the shop
each week. This includes all artwork shipped to customers, but it also
includes any artwork framed and handed over to Div 6 for a festival or gallery
event, or any original canvases completed and given to Erin to paint on. It
would NOT include any artwork that is produced and framed, but is then held
in the shop waiting to be shipped until the customer is ready to receive it.
This stat counts all particles LEAVING the workshop.
This metric is one that we have partial control over. We rely on sales to be up so that we have particles moving throughout the production line. This includes all items that are for both customers and the gallery, like the original canvases that Erin needs. If we are ensuring that customer orders are out on deadline and that all gallery items are getting out of the warehouse, we are able to keep this step up. If customer orders are low, we can always check in with Erin for original canvas orders or if anything is needed for the gallery. This allows us to see how many items are being completed each week and how we may be delegating our time. This stat also aligns with the warehouse operator steps, and we can use them as a comparison to see how much is leaving the warehouse in correlation to the number of warehouse operator steps being completed.
Warehouse Operator Steps is the number of steps taken to complete a battle plan in full. The steps are important points in the sequence of the production line that must be completed before moving on to the next step. For example, without working on the files, we would be unable to print the artwork that is needed for the collectors. A battle plan covers six pieces at a time. Six pieces is usually the optimal number of pieces to work on at a time. Each work area can hold about six
pieces on average, so it makes it easier to work on them in batches. You make six bases at a time, and then you have enough room on the tables to base six 3D prints, and also enough room to frame six pieces at a time, etc. After a battle plan has been completed the total steps are counted and logged.
This metric is something else that we have partial control over depending on the pieces that we have in the warehouse. We use this information to see how the time is being spent for warehouse operators. If production is high then steps will be high, but if production is low, then the steps will drop down. In a 40 hour work week we can complete 10 to 12 pieces of artwork and get them out of the warehouse based on the current times we have listed on the workshop list. This would average 50 to 60 steps completed each week. If this is not consistently being met while production is high, then we have to evaluate the warehouse operator and the time it is taking to complete these items as well. Without tracking these steps, we are unable to see how the production line is moving.

Cleaning the Artwork
There are several ways to clean the artwork once it has been returned, depending on the damage/dirt that is present. Once the piece is sent back, both the Production Manager and Erin will inspect the artwork before sending it to Artwork Services.
Some pieces will only need to be dusted lightly, while others will require a more thorough cleaning. Erin will help to determine what kind of cleaning is required for the artwork. Outlined below are the basic and more heavy cleanings.
BASIC CLEANING:
Hang the painting on the grid in the varnish room
Dust the painting with a brush or varnishing brush very lightly before beginning.
Wash your hands before beginning the cleaning process, and wear gloves as needed.
Grab a latex-free makeup sponge or Q-tip for cleaning and a cup of deionized water.
If using the latex-free makeup sponge, rinse it out with the deionized water before using it on the painting to remove any chemicals that may be present.
You will move in small sections, 1” x 1” or 2” x 2” depending on how dirty the piece is. The sponge and Q-tip only need to be slightly damp when cleaning the artwork. You will want to dab at the painting with the latex-free makeup sponge or move in small circular motions with the Q-tip.
Closely inspect each section as you move over the painting, noting any damage to the piece.
You will continue to gently clean the area until the latex-free makeup sponge or Q-tip is no longer dirty.
The piece will be slightly damp after (the canvas should not be wet though) and you will need to sit for at least an hour before beginning the varnishing process with the conservator varnish.
If you notice any chips in the artwork or other areas that are in need of repair, please alert Erin immediately so that she may touch up the piece as needed. This will have to dry before you are able to apply the conservator varnish to the piece.

Once the cleanser has been applied, let it sit for a moment (about 30 seconds) before moving back in with a new Q-tip to remove the cleanser and remove the build-up on the painting.
Closely inspect each section as you move over the painting, noting any damage to the piece.
CONDITIONAL: If the Kotton Kleanser is not working, you will want to go in with the Gainsborough Products super emulsion cleaner.

Grab a Q-tip and get some of the cleanser paste on the tip, and apply to the artwork.

Temporary Varnishing
Retouching Varnish: A temporary varnish that is applied to paintings that have recently dried. This will protect the painting as it continues to dry over the course of two years.
Prior to checking the artwork, check the varnish room to see if any prepped canvases need to be moved around. Move as needed and check with Erin to see if she will be prepping more. If she has already prepped a large amount, the varnish room will be free.
When an original is approaching their dry date, you will check the painting to see if it is dry, this will begin a week prior to the dry date. You will gently touch the painting to see if it is tacky or if areas with more paint are soft to the touch. Once the peaks are hard and the piece is no longer tacky, you will bring the piece to the varnishing room, located in the hallway leading to Building B. Always varnish when Erin is in the building in the event that something happens and she needs to touch up the artwork.
If the original is not dry yet, wait a day or two more or ask Erin for a second opinion if needed. It is best to wait for them to be as dry as possible before varnishing to ensure they do not get smudge during the process.
Tips:
Darker colors such as the greens and blues may take longer to dry
Yellow can also take longer to dry depending on how much is used
If there is a lot of white in the painting, the painting may dry faster
Erin will sometimes use a drier for her originals in order for them to be dry before a certain date, so these will be dry before the normal 8 week deadline
Once you have the original in the varnish room you will place the piece on the grid along the wall. Make sure it is stable and will not move as you are varnishing. You will bring the dry rack tag into the varnish room and place them on the table where you keep the varnishing supplies.
Now that the original is hung and ready to be varnished, you will need to put on gloves and a mask. The retouching varnishing liquid is an irritant and will cause your hands to break out and it is not good to breathe it in directly. Turn on the fan for the room. This will help get rid of some of the strong fumes that come with varnishing.

Once you have the cup, you will then grab your brush. If the brush has been previously used, you will need to check to see if the bristles are too stiff for varnishing. When demonstrating, the Production director will have you feel a used brush and a brand new brush to note the difference. If a brush is too stiff you will need to grab a new one to varnish with.
Prepping your supplies:
You will grab two paper towels and fold them in half vertically and then again horizontally so that you get a square
Add retouching varnishing liquid to the cups, just a small amount at first. You can always add more later.
Make sure to wipe off the opening of the bottle so that the cap does not stick
Grab the cup and paper towel in one hand and hold the brush in your other.

First, you will dip your brush in the varnish and brush off the excess on the paper towel.
You will dip the brush in the retouching varnishing liquid, gently wiping off some of the liquid.

Re-stretching original canvases
There are times when a collector purchases an original that is on a 2” or 1-½” stretcher and they would like to downsize it to fit into a different frame. This is not a common occurrence, but it will happen on occasion with Erin’s approval.
The process is extremely similar to stretching an original canvas, with just a more delicate piece you are working on. The piece can be varnished or unvarnished depending on when it was purchased by the collector.
The desired product is a canvas that is “drum-tight” and the original peaks and ridges are completely intact. You can actually tap the back of the stretched original and hear it make a drumming sound. And we don’t want any wrinkles in the original whatsoever. Wrinkles come from stretching the canvas without pulling it away from the center (see Step 10 below).
The following procedure is how to re-stretch an original canvas.
You will need to make sure that the foam is completely free of wood chips and any debris from stretching canvases.
Lay one of the bags that we use for shipping on the foam.
You will want to make sure that it is slightly larger than the piece that is being re-stretched.
Gently lay the original facedown on the bag to begin removing the staples from the canvas.
You will need to grab the pliers and the knife with the nail lifter attached. There is also a small flathead screw driver available to use for the removal of the staples.
When removing the staples make sure that you are not applying a lot of pressure to the front of the artwork.
You will place the flathead screwdriver or the nail lifter under the staple to gently pry out the staple before using the pliers to pull it out completely.
After each staple is removed it must be disposed of in the garbage. DO NOT leave the staples on the table or they will rip the original canvas.
Once all the staples are removed, you will remove the stretcher from the original.
Remove the tag from the stretcher frame and lay it on top of the original canvas. The curved ridge should be on the bottom.
Use a measuring tape and carpenter pencil to mark the center of the bar.
Position the stretcher in the center of the original canvas. Lift the edge of the canvas on the bottom, left, and right to ensure your corners are properly located. You will want to measure and ensure that there is about 2-½” on each side of the stretcher and the signature is not being pulled over the edge of the stretcher.
Place a single staple in the middle of the stretcher bar closest to you.
Turn the stretcher and original canvas 180°, very gently, ensure the stretcher is still centered from this angle, and using canvas pliers, place a single staple in the closest stretcher bar.
Turn the stretcher 90° (either left or right), ensure the stretcher is centered, and using canvas pliers, place a single staple in the closest stretcher bar.
Turn the stretcher and canvas 180°, ensure the stretcher is still centered from this angle, and using canvas pliers, place a single staple in the closest stretcher bar.
Turn the stretcher and canvas back to their original orientation, with the bottom of the original closest to you. Position the canvas pliers one length to the right of the indentation from the center of the canvas.
Stretch the original canvas with the pliers. Be careful not to pull too hard as this can cause the oil paint to crack on the original artwork. While you stretch forward, also twist the pliers so they pull the canvas away from the center of the original (this prevents wrinkles in the final print). Stretch it tight enough to make a drum sound, but not so tight that you rip the canvas.
Stretch and place two staples on the right-hand side.
Turn the canvas 180° and place two staples on the right-hand side.
Turn the canvas 90° and place one staple on the right-hand side, then turn the canvas 180° and place one staple on the right-hand side.
Now, “bounce back” the other direction, placing your staples on the left. Put two staples on the left of the side of the original now facing you.
Turn the canvas 180° and place two staples on the left-hand side.
Turn the canvas 90° and place two staples on the left-hand side, then turn the canvas 180° and place four staples on the left-hand side.  You can increase the amount of staples being placed on the longest side after the first two on each side of the original canvas, this would be two full rotations.
Return to the right-hand side and “bounce back” in the other direction again.
When the staples are equal distance from the corners, stop putting more staples on the longer edges of the original canvas, and only put two staples on each side when you “bounce” back and forth between left and right.
Keep stretching and stapling until you are about 2-3 inches away from the corners in all directions.
On the left and right sides of the image, put a final staple all the way at the end of the inside edge of the stretcher bar, and a final staple at the outside corner. Do this for the top and bottom of the original canvas.
Fold in the right-hand edge of the corner closest to you, folding the excess canvas underneath the fold. Place a staple on the bottom edge of the canvas strainer, holding the canvas in place without stapling through the canvas folded underneath.
With canvas scissors, cut the excess canvas in a right-hand angle as needed depending on how much of the canvas has already been cut from the first initial stretching prior to painting.
Fold the canvas up around the bottom of the original canvas, and with pliers, stretch it right and staple it in place.
Staple down any excess canvas that has popped up.
Repeat steps 20-24 for all four corners of the image.
Inspect the original after being stretched to check for tightness, wrinkles and how the original is centered on the canvas.
Place the completed re-stretched original canvas on the wire shelves with the bag that it was stretched on placed over it (ensures no dust gets on the original) in Unit B for final framing and wiring.

Once the piece is in place, you will plug in the engraver to the outlet, twist to the left on the emergency stop on the front of the machine and pull it forward, turn the key to the left and press in the reset button.

Now that the name has been changed, you will click on a box on the box left that says “Red” which will activate an outline from the laser where the engraving will go. Put on the eyewear for the engraver, the light that is emitted from the engraver can cause serious damage to your eyes. When everything is set and ready, you will hit the button next to “Red”, it is labelled “Mark”.

Turn the engraver off by pushing in the emergency stop and turning the key to the right.
Remove the nameplate from the engraver and place it on the table. You may have to wait a minute before grabbing the nameplate as it may be hot from engraving.

You may have to use one of the cotton swabs with some of the alcohol spray to really clean it off

After if has been cleaned off, you will make sure it it dry in preparation for adding the black acrylic paint
Apply a small amount of the black acrylic paint to a new cotton swab and gentle brush it over the newly engraved letters on the nameplate
Continue to scrub the acrylic with this cotton swab until you are able to remove most of the paint
With another new dry cotton swab remove the rest of the acrylic paint before doing a final cleaning with the cotton swab covered in alcohol
The nameplate can now have the double sided tape applied
Flip the nameplate over and apply the double sided tape as straight as possible from end to end and use the scissors to cut off the end
The nameplate is now ready to be attached to an original frame!

Once it is wrapped up, you will place it in a small baggy that is located under the engraving table.

Powder Coating Name Plates
We have a powder coating station set up in Unit A that we use for powder coating the nameplates that need a black surface with gold letting as preferred by Erin for the Black and Gold Plein Air frames.
Prepping the material:
Grab 10 - 20 gold nameplates and remove the masking tape cover off the face of them
Line the entire tray with aluminum foil so that none of the powder gets on the rack itself
Evenly space the nameplates onto the rack after applying the aluminum foil
Place the rack of nameplates into the oven
You will pre-bake the nameplates before applying the powder
Plug in the oven
Turn the temperature control knob to 400°F
Turn the element control knobs to the BOTH selection. This will allow for both the bottom and top heating elements to turn on
Turn the fan control knob to ON
Turn the timer control knob to 60 mins and allow the nameplates to heat to 400°F for the duration of the timer

Allow the nameplates to cool completely before handling them
Unplug the oven as soon as you are done with it

Apply a some of the chosen cleaning solution to a wipe and then clean all the nameplates
Place the nameplates back on the rack as evenly spaced as possible
Wait for them to dry before applying the powder

Now that the material is prepped for the powder coating you will need to put on a new pair of gloves and put on a face mask.

Turn the fan on to a high to medium setting

You will take the the air compressor hose from the stretching table and bring it over to the powder coating area
Check the amount of powder in the cup that is attached to the powder gun, ideally you want this cup fill about 2” deep with powder
Gently remove the cup from the gun
Use a plastic spoon to move the powder from the main container to the powder gun cup
Clean the edges of the cup with a paper towel before placing back onto the gun
You will now plug in the gun to the outlet that you had the oven plugged into
Attach the grounding connecting to the wires of the spray booth, this will connect the current to the rack of parts we are powder coating
Attach the air compressor hose to the gun
Check the deflector on the nozzle of the gun
The farther the deflector is from the tip of the gun, the smaller the fan pattern
We want this powder to be in a concentrated area on the nameplates

You will grab the activation switch and hold it in one hand while operating the powder gun in the other
Hold the button down on the activation switch and pull the trigger to get the powder gun going
Spray the powder at all angles on the nameplates, you will want to rotate the rack (very gently to not move them at all) to get the other side of the nameplates
Once the powder has been applied, you will want to remove the air compressor hose and unplug the powder to the powder gun
Curing the powder onto the nameplates
Plug in the oven
Turn the temperature control knob to 400°F
Turn the element control knobs to the BOTH selection. This will allow for both the bottom and top heating elements to turn on
Turn the fan control knob to ON
Turn the timer control knob to 10 mins and allow the oven to preheat for 10 mins before placing the nameplates into the oven
The power indicator will illuminate after the timer is set and the oven is activated.
After the timer goes off, place the rack of nameplates into the oven
Set the timer to 15 minutes
During this time you will check the nameplates every 5 minutes to see if the powder has reached its glossy liquid state (this is also called the “flow out”)

Set the timer to 20 minutes after the pieces have reached the “flow out” stage in the process
After the time has ended, pull the rack from the oven and place it on the rack of the spray booth
Allow the nameplates to cool completely before handling them
Unplug the oven as soon as you are done with it

Cutting Dibond Instructions
Dibond: Dibond is a brand of aluminum composite material that's used for a variety of applications, including signage, art, and furniture.
The Erin Hanson Gallery uses Dibond to print 3D replicas of Erin’s work.
This material comes in extremely large sheets, 60x120”, which then need to be cut down to the correct size of the 3D replica that we are going to create. This is cut on our CNC (Computer Numerical Control) machine. This machine allows us to input the correct dimensions for the piece we need to cut and it will use a sharp drill bit to then cut it out.
Place the sheet of dibond on the CNC machine.
There is a screen that is located next to the CNC machine, this controls the machine itself outside of the program that is used on the computer. If the screen is black, tap on it once to wake it up. Once the screen has lit up, tap the top left corner to access the control panel.
The head of the machine is located on a bar that can move over the table of the machine. This head emanates a laser + onto the table. You will be lining up the dibond sheet with this laser.
First you will press “To Zero” this will orient the machine to the starting point. You will then move the dibond sheet so that the corner is under the laser but it's just on the edge of the dibond sheet.
Using the large arrow keys on the screen, press the up arrow key to move the head up the table. Follow the laser as it is moving, you will be checking if the top of the dibond sheet is aligned with the laser.
After aligning the vertical edge, you will be aligning the horizontal edge.
Press “To Zero” to bring the laser down to the bottom right corner. Adjust the corner as needed to be aligned with the laser.
After checking the corner, press the left arrow to move the head to the bottom left corner of the dibond sheet. You will have to press the smaller right arrow key to move the laser over slightly to correct fit on the horizontal edge of the dibond sheet.
Lastly, you will press “To Zero” again to make sure the bottom right corner is lined up correctly. Repeat this process as needed until you are confident the dibond sheet is aligned correctly with the machine head.
Once the dibond sheet is correctly lined up, you will move the computer next to the machine. From the computer, you will enter the coordinates of the piece you will be cutting into the program that will then send the coordinates to the machine.
First you will open a google sheet titled “Packing Sheet” in this sheet you will be going to the “CNC boxes” tab. In most cases, this will already be open on the computer as it is frequently used. There is a lot of information located on this tab within the sheets, but we are only focusing on a small portion.
In this section, you will be entering the dimensions of the piece next to the “Dibond L (in.)” and “Dibond W (in.)”. During this explanation, I will be using the dimensions of 20x24 as an example.
After you enter these dimensions, the sheet will provide you with the coordinates that are needed for the CNC machine program. The sheet provides this information via the formulas that have been programmed into it.
Once you have the coordinates for the piece you are cutting out of the dibond (I recommend that you write these down on a sticky note), you will open a program called Xaio.This program will help you create the shape that you want to cut out from the dibond material. When you open the program, you will see the outline of the CNC machine. This will be your guide on how the piece will be cut from the dibond sheet.
Now that you have the program open, you will go to the tool bar at the top left of the screen. You will click on “Draw” which will then bring up a dropdown tab. You will select “rectangle”. This will then open a tab for you to input the coordinates that you got from the “Packing Sheet”.
When you open the dialog window for the rectangle, you will see the coordinates that were last used. The start coordinates will always be 0 and 0 for the first piece that you place in the program. This starting point aligns with the bottom right corner that you aligned with the laser on the machine. In the program this bottom right corner is located on the bottom left corner of the screen. Instead of looking at the CNC from a vertical position as when you are placing the sheet on the table, you are looking at the horizontal position in the program.
After changing the starting position for X and Y to 0, you will then place the end coordinates for the piece you are cutting out of the dibond. In my example of a 20x24, the coordinates would be 514x615.
Now that the coordinates are loaded for the rectangle, you will press “okay”. This will close the dialog window for the rectangle and bring you back to the starting screen for the program. This time, there will be a rectangle placed on the table. This is the piece that you will be cutting out.
In order to prevent the waste of any material, you will then add a line from the end of the piece to the outside edge of the dibond sheet. This will ensure that the next person to use this sheet, will have a clean starting point.
To create this line, you will need to go back to the “Draw” tab and this time you will select “line”. Once you have made this selection the dialog window for the line will open.
This time, the starting coordinates that you will enter will be the end coordinates from the window. These coordinates correspond to the outside of the top corner on the piece.
The X coordinate will remain the same as the starting point and the Y coordinate will extend to the point of 1525. This is the end of the dibond. Click “okay” and the dialog window for the line will close and the line will appear connected to the rectangle you have made.
The rectangle and line will need to be changed from light blue to a bright magenta color. Click on a space around the outside edge of the outline of the CNC machine, and then drag it so that a rectangle encompasses both the rectangle you created and the line. Once you have done this and released the hold on the rectangle, the shape will then become bold. You will then go towards the top right of the screen where the numbers are located. Clock on number “6”, it is in the bright magenta shade we need.
Number 6 is the program that correlates to the type of tool we use to cut out the dibond material. If it does not get changed to this color, it will not work.
Next, you will have to optimize the path of the machine. This will make it cut the shape out in the most efficient pattern. You will need to click on the green and blue “O” located at the top right of the screen.
After the path has been optimized, then you will send it to the machine. To do this, you will need to click on the down arrow that is located at the top right of the screen.
The CNC machine will emit a loud beeping noise, and this signals that the machine has received the coordinates for the piece that needs to be cut out.
Going back to the screen next to the machine, you will first press the button that looks like two brackets [ ], this will map out the area that the machine will be cutting. This helps to ensure that the dibond has been laid out correctly for the cut. Now you will press “Repeat” which will start the machine.

It will take some time for the machine to complete the process, and once it is done, you will need to take the shop vacuum that is next to the machine and clean off the surface of the dibond.
The dibond that is needed for the 3D replica is now complete and only needs to be prepped for printing.
To prep the piece of dibond for the machine, you will need to remove the plastic film that is on both sides of the piece. After the film is removed, you will need to sand the edges of the piece for a smooth finish. This will prevent the ink that is used for the 3D Replicas from sticking to any of the fibers of plastic from the dibond.
Cleaning Surface Instructions
Dibond: Dibond is a brand of aluminum composite material that's used for a variety of applications, including signage, art, and furniture.
The Erin Hanson Gallery uses Dibond to print 3D replicas of Erin’s work.
When we print 3D replicas, we always “oversize” each image slightly to ensure no Dibond shows on the edges of the print. This results in small strips of cured resin on the 3D printer’s surface.
The first step in cleaning the 3D printer surface is to scrape this resin off with a razor blade tool. This tool is stored in the stand-up computer station next to the 3D printer.
Thoroughly scrape all the resin off. There shouldn’t be any actual resin left on the table, and it should feel completely smooth to the touch. It’s normal that there might still be some dark grey or black discoloration after it’s scraped clean.
For the next step, put on protective gloves and spray the area with alcohol. An alcohol spray bottle is stored near the 3D printer. Then, use a magic sponge to thoroughly scrub the 3D printer surface. This should get rid of most of the discoloration left from the previous step.
Finally, spray the surface with alcohol again, and then thoroughly scrub it with a microfiber cloth. This will get rid of any remaining discoloration or streaks. You should end up with a perfectly clean, shiny metal surface.
Daily Maintenance and Set-up Instructions
There are 4 variations of the daily maintenance and set-up schedule for the 3D Printer. This schedule is important to maintain the working condition of the printer, without this maintenance we would not be able to print the 3D Textured Replicas. The swissQprint company provides an annual maintenance that is scheduled between May and July of each year. The printer is down for two days, and in order to keep our cost low for this maintenance and to not create extra work for the crew that comes out we follow a strict maintenance schedule ourselves.
The first schedule is for the times we have low production and we do not have orders to print. This schedule is for Tuesday through Friday, and it involves cleaning and a small sample print to get the printer up and running during these low periods. The second schedule is similar but has the weekly tasks that need to be completed so that the printer does not have any issues over the weekend and is in good condition to run on Tuesday the following week.
The third schedule is both our maintenance and set-up of the machine for printing. Like the first schedule, this one is outlined for Tuesday through Friday and contains all the basic information to get the printer up and running for a full print table. The fourth schedule is like the third but involves the weekly cleaning that has to be completed.
Repairs for this printer are expensive and time consuming as we have to call out the crew from swissQprint to come out and replace parts if we cannot fix it ourselves. This can cause a complete stop in the warehouse, without the prints, there’s nothing for our Warehouse Operators to work on. Our deadline will tank and our customers will be unhappy with the delays.
You will decide what schedule needs to be followed depending on the day of the week and what is scheduled for the printer. Print this off and follow each step, checking the boxes as you go. Once the schedule has been completed, it will be signed at the bottom and filed away in the 3D Print Binder.

Ink Change List
When we change out one of the ink on the printer, this data gives us an idea of what inks we are using and how fast we are going through them.
Ink Usage Log
Each day we are logging what the ink levels are for each of the inks in the printer. This data is graphed out and correlated with how many prints are made. We can see what inks are being consumed at higher volumes depending on how many pieces are being printed.
Print Log
This log is to document what is being printed, how many are being printed and what has been approved by Erin.
Ink Inventory
We keep a log of when we receive the inks for the printer as well as their expiration date. These inks do expire and we can no longer use them in the printer when that happens, as it will clog the print heads which will damage them.
Monthly Maintenance
We do have some maintenance tasks that need to be completed each month, it is a significantly smaller list
3D Print Room Temperature Check
The room needs to maintain a temperature between 65°F and 85°F. If the room drops below or rises above these temperatures, it can cause damage to the printer. It needs to be regulated throughout the year.

DATE: __________________________
Beginning of the day
Turn on printer computer
Press blue power button on printer
Password for the computer is: swissQprint
Turn on printer
Turn dial that is located under the blue power button to the right
Press the ↲ on the computer table to initialize the machine
Press the ■ on the computer table to turn on the printer, this will connect it to the computer
Open Amber once the printer is turned on
Clean off print table (detailed in Cleaning the 3D Printer Surface)
Scrape off ink with a blade
Vacuum ink particles off the printer
Spray alcohol on ink residue and then scrub with the magic eraser
Spray alcohol and use microfiber cloth to wipe away streak marks on printer
Clean off printer with the air compressor
Sweep/Vacuum around the 3D Printer and dibond cutter
Remove particles from the 3D Printer with the air compressor
Log what each of the ink levels are at on the "Ink Usage Log"
Place the foam boards on the printer (2) and make sure the vents are closed except for under the foam boards. Once the boards are placed on the table, make sure that the material height under the printer icon is set to 5.7.
Open Amber
Click the “Calibration” tab
Click “Standby” and wait for printer to move to cleaning position
Click “Service” under the white ink color
Click "All On" to turn all of the inks of if they have not turned on by themselves
Hold Shift and click “Purge,” holding the click until the arrow moves back up to “On”.
All ink buckets will turn yellow, wait for them to turn green before continuing
Clean print heads with print head wipe soaked in flush
Clean the print heads with a dry print head wipe
Run a nozzle check
If any nozzles are missing or deflected, run an addition purging and then dry wipe or wipe with flush
Repeat nozzle check again, continue to clean if nozzles are missing or deflected
If you have cleaned the nozzles three times with little to no change, use the alcohol soaked wipe to gently clean the head
Wipe the head with flush after it has been cleaned with the alcohol
Dry with a wipe before running another nozzle check
Once the nozzle check is clear, print the rulers back on the table if any are missing
Setting up the sample print on the table
Place the sample piece on the table, make sure that it is pressed against the side ruler
Depending on how much of the table you are using, you will go to "Orientation" tab and select "front only"
You will turn the vacuum on once the dibond is set on the table. You will check the lights on the table to see if you need to add any black sheets of paper to cover the holes to better secure the pieces to the table. The lights will turn green when the table is completely set and the pieces are secured.
When everything is secured, you will go to the "Main" tab and select the folder for the sample text.
Opening the folder you will select "Process" and hit print. A window will pop up after hitting print and you will set the material height to 3.45.
Printing the sample piece
Once the Sample Text has been printed, flip over the dibond like you're turning a page.
On the "Main" tab and select the sample file containing the print. Select the entire folder and hit print. Ensure that the material height is still set to 3.45 and then you will need to hit okay for all of the layers.
Watch the table through the first layer to make sure everything is running smoothly
After a table of texture prints
Clean the section of the table that is used for the nozzle check
Place the foam boards on the printer (2) and make sure the vents are closed except for under the foam boards. Once the boards are placed on the table, make sure that the material height under the printer icon is set to 5.7.
Open Amber
Click the “Calibration” tab
Click “Standby” and wait for printer to move to cleaning position
Click “Service” under the white ink color
Hold Shift and click “Purge,” holding the click until the arrow moves back up to “On”.
All ink buckets will turn yellow, wait for them to turn green before continuing
Clean print heads with print head wipe soaked in flush
Clean the print heads with a dry print head wipe
Run a nozzle check
If any nozzles are missing or deflected, run an addition purging and then dry wipe or wipe with flush
If you have cleaned the nozzles three times with little to no change, use the alcohol soaked wipe to gently clean the head
Wipe the head with flush after it has been cleaned with the alcohol
Dry with a wipe before running another nozzle check
Repeat nozzle check again, continue to clean if nozzles are missing or deflected
End of the day
Clean the glasses of the UV lamps
Place the foam boards on the printer (2) and make sure the vents are closed except for under the foam boards. Once the boards are placed on the table, make sure that the material height under the printer icon is set to 5.7.
Open Amber
Run a nozzle check
If any nozzles are missing or deflected, run an addition purging and then dry wipe or wipe with flush
Repeat nozzle check again, continue to clean if nozzles are missing or deflected
Clean baseplate with a print head wipe and flush
Wipe all print heads with flush
Click the “Calibration” tab
Click “Standby” and wait for printer to move to cleaning position
Click “Service” under the white ink color
Hold Shift and click “Purge,” holding the click until the arrow moves back up to “On”.
Close Amber
Turn off PC
Turn off printer

Download the 3D Print Files
Open the "Finder" application on the Caldera computer (there should be 5 windows open)
Go to the bottom right to the "Hot Folder" and you are going to delete the file folders for the pieces that were printed already.
After the file folders have been deleted, you will move the merged files to the folder labelled "Past Prints".
First check the Production Tags for what you will be printing for the day, it should be separated into two piles. One pile for pieces that have the files already complete and then one pile for the files that still need to be merged.
First prepare the files that already have files that are already to complete.
You will check the middle finder window on the right side, looking at the "00 3D Prints Library" for the files that have already been merged. Once located, move them into the "Hot Folder".
Next, you will move onto the pile of Production Tags that still need to be processed and merged.
You will first delete the folders that are located in "00 Prints to Process" on the bottom left.
The first window on the left will be open to "TIFF Scans"
You will locate the files that you need to print and bring it to "00 Prints to Process" which will be open on the left just under the first window. You will click and drag the file over to "00 Prints to Process" so that it can begin copying there.
While the files are copying into that folder, you will first look at the top right finder window "ImgWorkDir" this is where the files are processed in the Caldara program, they should automatically delete from this folder when they are done processing but sometimes they do not. You will want to delete any files that did not self delete before moving on. DO NOT DELETE THE FOLDERS IN THIS WINDOW. Only delete the individual files as needed.
Now all folders are prepped, and the files you moved from "TIFF Scans" to "00 Prints to Process" should be downloaded. You will move to the second screen by hitting control and the right arrow key.
The Caldara program will be open as well as the other Finder windows (There should be 4 in total)
The "00 Prints to Process" window will be in the same location and you will open the file folder that you wish to print so that you can see the individual .tif files within the folder.
Once the file is open you will first grab the color files labelled as "LR Diffuse" or "shaded" and move it into the top window "EH Painting Color workflow WH + V". This is where the file is processed for the white and varnish.
Next you will grab the other 15 files (0-14) and move them to the bottom finder window "EH Texture Layer Workflow". This is where the texture slices are processed.
The folders for the prints will now be empty after transferring the information to be processed. You will now move back to the main screen with control and the left arrow key.
You will now click and drag the empty folders into from "00 Prints to Process" to the "hot folder", and then number them. (example: 01 Fjord Reflections 24x30 HORI 2026-03-18)
You will just have to wait for the files to process through the program and download into the "hot folder"

Once in "Merge files to layer" you will select the folder that you want to merge.
You will first add the color file, then in descending order, you will add slices 14-0.
Double check that all the files have been added together, then hit "okay". You will use the name of the folder (without the number 01) to name the now merged files.

You will now go into "shadow files" tab and "remove all files"
After removing the previous files, you will "Add new file" and select the first piece you want to add. You will need to put in the correct coordinates that match with where the piece is placed on the table
When all the files have been added you will return to the "Main" tab and select the sample file containing the print. Select the entire folder and hit print. Ensure that the material height is still set to 3.45 and then you will need to hit okay for all of the layers.

Cleaning Printer Insturctions
First you will need to wake the printer up, which may take up to five minutes. You can do this by pressing the “Ok” button on the machine.
After the machine has woken from sleep mode, we will need to run a nozzle check
There are three buttons above the screen on the printer. You will press the button that is to the far right, it will have an “A” with lines through it pointing to a solid “A”
Once you press that button, a menu will appear, and you will select “Nozzle Check”. It should be highlighted yellow, indicating that it is the selection you are choosing. To select this you will press “Ok”.
After selecting okay, it will prompt you to “Press ‘Ok’ to print the nozzle check pattern”, press “Ok” again to begin the process.
It should take less than a minute for the nozzle check to happen.
After the nozzle check has been printed, you will take a moment to look it over on the canvas. You will check to see if there are any holes in the color. If there are, you will need to clean the heads and run another nozzle check.
After the nozzle check is completed, you will be prompted with “Clean print head if there is faint or missing segment in the check pattern”. Two options are available with this prompt, “Exit” and “Head Cleaning”. Press the arrow down to select “Head Cleaning” by pressing “Ok”.

You will use the arrow keep to press down and select “Start Cleaning” by pressing “Ok”.
You will now be prompted to select “Nozzle & Level”, the standard is to select “Normal” by pressing “Ok”.
After selecting “Normal” a loading bar will appear on the screen indicating how far along the cleaning process is. This will take about 5 minutes, I recommend setting a timer on your phone to check on it while it is cleaning.
Once the cleaning process is complete, you will be prompted to run another nozzle check, which you will select.
Check the nozzle check again and confirm that all heads are running as they should
CONDITIONAL: If there are still missing heads, run another normal head cleaning and then nozzle check until there are no missing heads
CONDITIONAL: If you repeat the sequence three times and there are still missing heads, schedule maintenance for the printer immediately
Once the nozzle check is no longer missing, proceed with starting a head alignment.
You will go to the same button as was used to get to the nozzle check, but you will use the arrow key to click down until you can select “Head Alignment” by pressing “Ok”.
After this you will be prompted to select the type of head alignment the machine will run, you will select “Auto (Bi-D) head alignment”
This process will take approximately 10 minutes, I recommend setting a timer on your phone.

Delivery Coordination
19051114300
In the third section of Artwork Services we have Delivery Coordination. You will be responsible for handling all of the coordination between customers and the gallery for delivery. This means contacting original collectors when their artwork is ready to be shipped, or contacting the 3D textured replica and canvas print collectors to arrange delivery for their artwork according to their needs.
You will receive all the details regarding deliveries which will then be passed to the warehouse. The Production Director will send emails regarding any changes to orders that may have been made as well as ensuring that red notes are included in all of these orders. The red notes will notify you that something on the order has been changed.
With this information, you will be able to create the daily Battle Plans (BPs) for the warehouse to ensure that everything is delivered on deadline. This area focuses on some of your most important stats as the Production Director. The VSD is the value of services delivered into the hands of the customer, which means ensuring that all artwork is being delivered into the hands of the customers in a timely manner. You are responsible for keeping up the percentage of particles sent out on deadline. We guarantee our customers will receive notifications that their artwork has been shipped out 14 calendar days after the order has been received (this timeline varies depending on the type of artwork that is being sent out). In order to meet that guarantee, you will be arranging the BPs for all persons working in the warehouse as Warehouse Operators. We want to be working at least one week ahead of the current deadline.
Some of the deliveries will require special services, such as white glove deliveries or different courier services. You will be the one to contact these services and arrange for the delivery of the artwork.
Definitions:
Value of Services Delivered (VSD) is the dollar amount of artwork (originals, 3D
prints, canvas prints) delivered to customers that week. To count as Services
Delivered, the artwork must be confirmed to have reached the customer. For
prints, FedEx confirmation is acceptable. For originals, we require the
customer’s signature on the order.
Orders Shipped on Deadline is a percentage stat. There are various
deadlines to get an order shipped (3 days for originals, 14 days for prints,
etc., and all modified if the customer wishes to put a delay on the order). The percentage of orders shipped on deadline in a week gives you this stat. If an
item is over deadline, it is counted as a negative on the weekly stat every
week it is in the shop, not just the week it finally gets shipped out.
Particles Handled is the number of finished products that leave the shop
each week. This includes all artwork shipped to customers, but it also
includes any artwork framed and handed over to Div 6 for a festival or gallery
event, or any original canvases completed and given to Erin to paint on. It
would NOT include any artwork that is produced and framed, but is then held
in the shop waiting to be shipped until the customer is ready to receive it.
This stat counts all particles LEAVING the workshop.

Double Miters
Framing Double Miter
One of the double miters, the larger of the two, we use specifically for framing and framing alone. This double miter has specific blades that are made to handle the moulding that we use for framing. We are looking for consistent and clean cuts for our frames so they are able to join properly.
Cleaning
Daily:
Clean off the dust and debris from the entrance and exit of the double miter.
Ensure that none of the moulding material is blocking the exit shoot under the blades where the excess is dropped.
Empty the tray of excess pieces from the cuts that were made the previous day.
Weekly:
The blades need to be cleaned or changed on a weekly basis depending on the usage. If we are cutting more than 25 frames in the week, we will need to change the blades. This is roughly 100 cuts with the machine. If we are cutting less than 25 frames, we just need to clean the blades, making sure they are free of build-up.
Cleaning the blades:
Turn the machine off by unplugging it from the main power.
Remove the hood covering the blades.
Remove the blades from the machine.
Dust the bolts and washers that hold the blades in place, cleaning them off if necessary.
Grab a rag and some of the window cleaner, and gently clean the blade's edge.
Place the blades back in the machine and make sure they are tight.
Place the hood back over the blades and plug in the machine to the main power once more.
Changing the blades:
Turn the machine off by unplugging it from the main power.
Remove the hood covering the blades.
Remove the blades from the machine.
Dust the bolts and washers that hold the blades in place, cleaning them off if necessary.
Do not remove the wax from the new blades until they are secured in the machine. While tightening the bolts, your arms may get close to the blades.
Place the blades back in the machine and make sure they are tight, once they are secured, remove the blue wax before placing the hood back on and plugging in the machine.
Cleaning out the inside of the machine:
Ensure the power is off by unplugging the machine from the main power.
Remove the back panel from the machine.
Using one of the shop vacs, thoroughly clean the machine removing all of the sawdust.
Monthly:
General inspection of equipment, checking the cords and other areas around the machine.
Check the dust collector to make sure that it is in good condition.
Check if the dust collector bag needs to be replaced.

Cardboard CNC
We have two different CNC Machines set up in Unit C, one for cardboard and one for our dibond and ABS. These machines are the same but have different cutting tools installed. Each of them has a computer that controls them and designates what they are to cut within the program.
The maintenance for these machines is fairly minimal, which will be detailed below. The cardboard CNC machine has a blade and roller for creating the cuts and the scoring for the folds for the boxes. The roller does not need to be replaced, just ensure the area is clean, but the blade does need to be replaced as needed. There is a sacrificial mat that is on top of the machine that separates the material being cut and the machine. This mat is specifically designed to allow the vacuum to hold the material down while still being able to protect the machine.

Changing the blade:
Send the CNC head to zero
Unplug the control cable that runs from the blade holder to the control head
Pull down on the black blade guard head to remove it from the blade
Unscrew the holding bracket and turn the blade holder counter-clockwise and lift it to remove it from the control head
Using allen keys in the nearby tool kit, remove the screws holding the blade in place
Remove the old blade and replace it with a new one, ensuring the blade oriented in the same direction as the old one
Using the allen keys, re-insert the screws holding the blade in place
Put the blade holder back in the control head and secure it with the holding bracket
On the “adjust” screen of the CNC touchpad, move the blade head down until it is just touching the surface of the sacrificial mat, then hit “Set” to set the new blade height
Do a test cardboard cut to ensure the blade height is correctly set, adjusting the blade height as needed
Reattach the black blade guard to the blade

Warehouse Stations
Building B is split into three different units: Unit A, Unit B, and Unit C. They are set up in correlation with the production line, moving from the left of the building to the right. Each section is designed to flow into the other, and there are different stations within each unit for the specific jobs that need to be completed. These stations correspond to the steps on our workshop list.
Each station must be cleaned at the end of each day to ensure that the warehouse is operating at the highest efficiency. If a station is left messy and disorganized, that will create problems for the next day where you will have to spend your time cleaning up the equipment before starting on your tasks. If a station is left clean and in order, you can check over the equipment and get started on your tasks for the day. We are working to create a cohesive and efficient environment without wasted motion.

Bases Instructions
Rabbet: In American parlance, rabbet is "The recess or groove of a frame for holding the picture". Or "The groove under the lip of the moulding that allows space for the mat, glass, art and mounting board." Rabbet is also found in woodworking.
Because 3D prints are printed on flat Dibond, they need to be raised up to sit correctly in the floater frames most commonly used in The Erin Hanson Gallery.
These bases are built out of pine board and painted black on the outside edges so as not to be noticed when sitting in the floater frames.
We build three kinds of bases: those for EH frame moulding, those for REH frame moulding, and those for regular floater frames. EH frames are taller, so their bases need to be taller. And EH frames have a deeper rabbet, so the base needs to be a little shorter in length.
The specifications are as follows:
EH Frames:
BASE HEIGHT:1 3/8” TALL
BASE LENGTH:PAINTING LENGTH - 3/4”
REH Frames:
BASE HEIGHT:1 1/4” TALL
BASE LENGTH:PAINTING LENGTH - 1 1/2”
Regular Frames:
BASE HEIGHT:1 3/16” TALL
BASE LENGTH:PAINTING LENGTH - 1/2”
First, rip enough lengths of pine board on the table saw to assemble your base. Set the ripping guide to 1⅜” for an EH base, 1 1/4” for an REH base, or 1 3/16” for a regular base.
On the green double miter saw, set the length for the longest dimension of your piece. Subtract ¾” for an EH base or ½” for a regular base. Use the clear plastic guide on top of the jig on the double miter saw to correctly gauge the length. Cut two lengths of pine board.
Repeat Step 2, but for the shortest dimension of your piece.
With tape, attach a Post-it or printed frame tag to one of the shorter lengths. The tag must list the Customer Name, Piece Name, Size, and Frame, as shown.
On the joiner, join the base corners with glue and 12mm v-nails. Use two stacked staples near the back edge, two stacked staples near the front edge, and two stacked staples in the center of each corner.
If the piece’s longest dimension is more than 30 inches, cut a center spreader. Do this by measuring the gap between the two longest pieces of the base. Cut a length of base board to this length on the chop saw.
Attach this center spreader in the middle of the base with glue and two brad nails on each side. It should be flush with the top of the base.
If the piece’s longest dimension is more than 45 inches, cut corner spreaders on the green double miter saw. These should be 12” in length and oriented to stand as tall as the rest of the base. They should be flush with the top of the base.
Attach these corner spreaders at the corners of the base with glue and two brad nails on each side.
Hang the base on the base painting rack.
Paint the outside edge of the base black with a single good, solid coat.

Original Canvas Stretchers Instructions
Stretcher bar: A stretcher bar is used to construct a wooden stretcher used by artists to mount their canvases. They are traditionally a wooden framework support on which an artist fastens a piece of canvas.
The vast majority of Erin’s original artwork is painted on canvas, which is mounted on canvas stretchers that are built in the workshop. These must be expertly made, as the canvas (and Erin’s painting) are expected to last much longer than the lifetime of the collector who purchases it. The following procedure is how we create the canvas stretchers for these prints.
On a WHITE Post-It, create a tag for the stretcher. The tag must list:
The canvas size
The word “ORIG”
On the green double-miter saw, set the cutting guide to the length of the longer dimension of the canvas, minus 1/16 of an inch. For example, if the longer dimension is 36”, you would set the cutting guide to 3515/16 of an inch. Use the clear plastic panel on top of the guide to set the correct length.
Repeat Step 2, but for the shortest dimension of your piece.
With tape, attach the blue Post-It to one of the shorter lengths.
On the joiner, join the base corners with glue and 12mm v-nails. Use two stacked staples near the back edge, two stacked staples near the front edge, and two stacked staples in the center of each corner.
Cut a center strainer. Do this by measuring the gap between the two longest pieces of the base. Cut a length of strainer board to this length on the chop saw. (Strainer board is stored near the stretcher bars, but it is thinner). NOTE: Original canvases ALWAYS get a center strainer, regardless of size. This is opposed to canvas prints, which only get a center strainer if the piece has a dimension of over 30”.
Attach this center strainer in the middle of the base with glue and two brad nails on each side. It should be flush with the bottom of the stretcher and oriented to lay flat.
Cut corner strainers on the green double miter saw. These should be 12” in length and oriented to lay flat. NOTE: Original canvases ALWAYS get corner strainers.
Attach these corner strainers at the corners of the stretcher, with glue and two brad nails on each side. They should be flush with the bottom of the stretcher.
If the canvas’ longest dimension is over 60”, cut two length-wise strainers to be attached to the center strainer. Do this by measuring the gap between the center strainer, and the short dimensions of the canvas stretcher.
Attach the length-wise strainers to the center strainer, and to the short ends of the canvas stretcher, with glue and two brad nails on each side. They should be flush with the bottom of the stretcher and oriented to lay flat.
Sand the corners of the stretcher so they don’t tear the canvas during stretching.
Place the stretcher in Unit A, leaning against the wall by the door, resting on the foam.
Canvas Prints Stretcher Instructions
Stretcher bar: A stretcher bar is used to construct a wooden stretcher used by artists to mount their canvases. They are traditionally a wooden framework support on which an artist fastens a piece of canvas.
The Erin Hanson gallery reproduces Erin Hanson’s work in two ways:
3D replicas, which are printed with cured UV resin and have a heavy texture
Canvas prints, which are printed on canvas with no texture.
Canvas prints are hand-stretched over wooden stretcher frames, just like the canvases Erin uses to paint her original artworks on. The following procedure is how we create the canvas stretchers for these prints.
From the workshop list, create a tag for the stretcher. This can be printed out, or it can be written with Sharpie on a Post-it note. In either case, the tag must list:
The customer name
The print name
The print size
The type of frame the print will go in
On the green double-miter saw, set the cutting guide to the length of the longer dimension of the art piece, minus 1/16 of an inch. For example, if the longer dimension is 36”, you would set the cutting guide to 35 15/16th of an inch. Use the clear plastic panel on top of the guide to set the correct length.
On the green double miter saw, set the length for the longest dimension of your piece. Subtract 1/16”. Use the clear plastic guide on top of the jig on the double miter saw to correctly gauge the length. Cut two lengths of stretcher bar.

With tape, attach the tag to one of the shorter lengths.

Stretching a Canvas Print instructions
Canvas prints are hand-stretched over wooden stretcher frames. The edges of the print are colored black, to be as unobtrusive as possible whether the print is in a frame or hung without one. A black cloth backing is then attached to the back of the canvas to hide the inside of its stretcher, and a print certificate is adhered to the cloth.
Before you perform the steps in this section, ask the Production Director to show you a stretched canvas piece so you have an idea of what you’re going to learn to make.
The desired product is a canvas that is “drum-tight.” You can actually tap the stretched canvas and hear it make a drumming sound. And we don’t want any wrinkles in the canvas whatsoever. Wrinkles come from stretching the canvas without pulling it away from the center (see Step 10 below).
The following procedure is how to stretch a canvas print.
Lay the canvas print facedown on the foam that covers the canvas stretching table. Position one of the long edges of the image towards yourself.
Remove the tag from the stretcher frame and lay it on top of the canvas print. The curved ridge should be on the bottom.
For any stretcher bar that isn’t attached to a center strainer, use a measuring tape and carpenter pencil to mark the center of the bar.
Position the stretcher in the center of the canvas. Lift the edge of the canvas on the bottom, left, and right to ensure your corners are properly located. About ⅛” of the image should be wrapping around the stretcher and showing on all three sides.
Place a single staple in the middle of the stretcher bar closest to you.
Turn the stretcher and canvas 180°, ensure the stretcher is still centered from this angle, and using canvas pliers, place a single staple in the closest stretcher bar.
Turn the stretcher 90° (either left or right), ensure the stretcher is centered, and using canvas pliers, place a single staple in the closest stretcher bar.
Turn the stretcher and canvas 180°, ensure the stretcher is still centered from this angle, and using canvas pliers, place a single staple in the closest stretcher bar.
Turn the stretcher and canvas back to their original orientation, with the bottom of the image closest to you. Position the canvas pliers one length to the right of the indentation from the center of the canvas.
Stretch the canvas with the pliers. While you stretch forward, also twist the pliers so they pull the canvas away from the center of the image (this prevents wrinkles in the final print). Stretch it tight enough to make a drum sound, but not so tight that you rip the canvas.
Stretch and place two staples on the right-hand side.
Turn the canvas 180° and place two staples on the right-hand side.
Turn the canvas 90° and place one staple on the right-hand side, then turn the canvas 180° and place one staple on the right-hand side.
Now, “bounce back” the other direction, placing your staples on the left. Put two staples on the left of the side of the image now facing you.
Turn the canvas 180° and place two staples on the left-hand side.
Turn the canvas 90° and place four staples on the left-hand side, then turn the canvas 180° and place four staples on the left-hand side.
Return to the right-hand side and “bounce back” in the other direction again.
When the staples are equal distance from the corners, stop putting more staples on the longer edges of the image, and only put two staples on each side when you “bounce” back and forth between left and right.
Keep stretching and stapling until you are about 2-3 inches away from the corners in all directions.
On the left and right sides of the image, put a final staple all the way at the end of the inside edge of the stretcher bar, and a final staple at the outside corner. Do this for the top and bottom of the image.
Fold in the right-hand edge of the corner closest to you, folding the excess canvas underneath the fold (see the image). Place a staple on the bottom edge of the canvas strainer, holding the canvas in place without stapling through the canvas folded underneath.
With canvas scissors, cut the excess canvas in a right-hand angle (see the image).
Fold the canvas up around the bottom of the image, and with pliers, stretch it right and staple it in place.
Staple down any excess canvas that has popped up.
Repeat steps 20-24 for all four corners of the image.
Apply two rows of rolling tape to all four sides of the back of the canvas print.
Place the canvas face-up on top of the black cloth used for backing. Press it down firmly to adhere the cloth to the print.
Cut the cloth from the roll and turn the canvas over face-down. Staple the closest left corner of the cloth into the back of the print.
Go around the print in a circle, stretching out the cloth so it is taut and held in place by the rolling glue. Once each side has been stretched, staple the cloth to the corner just like the first corner.
Using a razor blade, trim all the excess cloth from the back of the print. Ensure you are using a SHARP razor for a perfect cut.
Apply rolling tape to the back of a canvas print certificate sticker and apply it to the bottom-right corner of the back of the print.
Place the completed print on the wire shelves in Unit B for final framing and wiring.

Training Instructions
Dibond: Dibond is a brand of aluminum composite material that's used for a variety of applications, including signage, art, and furniture.
This material comes in extremely large sheets, 60x120”, which then need to be cut down to the correct size of the 3D replica that we are going to create. This is cut on our CNC (Computer Numerical Control) machine. This machine allows us to input the correct dimensions for the piece we need to cut and it will use a sharp drill bit to then cut it out.
Place the sheet of dibond on the CNC machine.
There is a screen that is located next to the CNC machine, this controls the machine itself outside of the program that is used on the computer. If the screen is black, tap on it once to wake it up. Once the screen has lit up, tap the top left corner to access the control panel.
The head of the machine is located on a bar that can move over the table of the machine. This head emanates a laser + onto the table. You will be lining up the dibond sheet with this laser.
First you will press “To Zero” this will orient the machine to the starting point. You will then move the dibond sheet so that the corner is under the laser but it's just on the edge of the dibond sheet.
Using the large arrow keys on the screen, press the up arrow key to move the head up the table. Follow the laser as it is moving, you will be checking if the top of the dibond sheet is aligned with the laser.
After aligning the vertical edge, you will be aligning the horizontal edge.
Press “To Zero” to bring the laser down to the bottom right corner. Adjust the corner as needed to be aligned with the laser.
After checking the corner, press the left arrow to move the head to the bottom left corner of the dibond sheet. You will have to press the smaller right arrow key to move the laser over slightly to correct fit on the horizontal edge of the dibond sheet.
Lastly, you will press “To Zero” again to make sure the bottom right corner is lined up correctly. Repeat this process as needed until you are confident the dibond sheet is aligned correctly with the machine head.
Once the dibond sheet is correctly lined up, you will move the computer next to the machine. From the computer, you will enter the coordinates of the piece you will be cutting into the program that will then send the coordinates to the machine.
First you will open a google sheet titled “Packing Sheet” in this sheet you will be going to the “CNC boxes” tab. In most cases, this will already be open on the computer as it is frequently used. There is a lot of information located on this tab within the sheets, but we are only focusing on a small portion.
In this section, you will be entering the dimensions of the piece next to the “Dibond L (in.)” and “Dibond W (in.)”. During this explanation, I will be using the dimensions of 20x24 as an example.
After you enter these dimensions, the sheet will provide you with the coordinates that are needed for the CNC machine program. The sheet provides this information via the formulas that have been programmed into it.
Once you have the coordinates for the piece you are cutting out of the dibond (I recommend that you write these down on a sticky note), you will open a program called Xaio.This program will help you create the shape that you want to cut out from the dibond material. When you open the program, you will see the outline of the CNC machine. This will be your guide on how the piece will be cut from the dibond sheet.
Now that you have the program open, you will go to the tool bar at the top left of the screen. You will click on “Draw” which will then bring up a dropdown tab. You will select “rectangle”. This will then open a tab for you to input the coordinates that you got from the “Packing Sheet”.
When you open the dialog window for the rectangle, you will see the coordinates that were last used. The start coordinates will always be 0 and 0 for the first piece that you place in the program. This starting point aligns with the bottom right corner that you aligned with the laser on the machine. In the program this bottom right corner is located on the bottom left corner of the screen. Instead of looking at the CNC from a vertical position as when you are placing the sheet on the table, you are looking at the horizontal position in the program.
After changing the starting position for X and Y to 0, you will then place the end coordinates for the piece you are cutting out of the dibond. In my example of a 20x24, the coordinates would be 514x615.
Now that the coordinates are loaded for the rectangle, you will press “okay”. This will close the dialog window for the rectangle and bring you back to the starting screen for the program. This time, there will be a rectangle placed on the table. This is the piece that you will be cutting out.
In order to prevent the waste of any material, you will then add a line from the end of the piece to the outside edge of the dibond sheet. This will ensure that the next person to use this sheet, will have a clean starting point.
To create this line, you will need to go back to the “Draw” tab and this time you will select “line”. Once you have made this selection the dialog window for the line will open.
This time, the starting coordinates that you will enter will be the end coordinates from the window. These coordinates correspond to the outside of the top corner on the piece.
The X coordinate will remain the same as the starting point and the Y coordinate will extend to the point of 1525. This is the end of the dibond. Click “okay” and the dialog window for the line will close and the line will appear connected to the rectangle you have made.
The rectangle and line will need to be changed from light blue to a bright magenta color. Click on a space around the outside edge of the outline of the CNC machine, and then drag it so that a rectangle encompasses both the rectangle you created and the line. Once you have done this and released the hold on the rectangle, the shape will then become bold. You will then go towards the top right of the screen where the numbers are located. Clock on number “6”, it is in the bright magenta shade we need.
Number 6 is the program that correlates to the type of tool we use to cut out the dibond material. If it does not get changed to this color, it will not work.
Next, you will have to optimize the path of the machine. This will make it cut the shape out in the most efficient pattern. You will need to click on the green and blue “O” located at the top right of the screen.
After the path has been optimized, then you will send it to the machine. To do this, you will need to click on the down arrow that is located at the top right of the screen.
The CNC machine will emit a loud beeping noise, and this signals that the machine has received the coordinates for the piece that needs to be cut out.
Going back to the screen next to the machine, you will first press the button that looks like two brackets [ ], this will map out the area that the machine will be cutting. This helps to ensure that the dibond has been laid out correctly for the cut. Now you will press “Repeat” which will start the machine.
π
It will take some time for the machine to complete the process, and once it is done, you will need to take the shop vacuum that is next to the machine and clean off the surface of the dibond.
The dibond that is needed for the 3D replica is now complete and only needs to be prepped for printing.
To prep the piece of dibond for the machine, you will need to remove the plastic film that is on both sides of the piece. After the film is removed, you will need to sand the edges of the piece for a smooth finish. This will prevent the ink that is used for the 3D Replicas from sticking to any of the fibers of plastic from the dibond.
